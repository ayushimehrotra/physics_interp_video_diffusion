What was measured, and on what

The 75 momentum-conserving scenes of probe_set v1 — the legacy arm, where mass is visible. Violating scenes are excluded because their aftermath was overwritten after the simulation, so post-contact kinematics there are not what the physics produced. Nothing here reads the conserved/violated label, so §38's leak is irrelevant.

The question is not is a factor decodable — that was settled long ago — but how the factors are arranged relative to each other. Ten factors: each ball's velocity, speed and direction, and each ball's mass and radius.

The label geometry is unusually clean, which is what makes this measurable

Across the 75 scenes every correlation between a kinematic and an appearance factor is under |0.31|. No subspace result below is driven by correlated labels. Two facts do constrain the design, and both are handled explicitly:

  • 24 of the 75 scenes have ball B at rest, so velocity_B has a point mass at the origin and direction is undefined there — §34a's concentric pathology in miniature. Every result was re-run on the 51 moving-B scenes.
  • Direction is 87% linearly predictable from velocity (measured, not assumed). Speed is 2%. So speed vs direction is a clean comparison and velocity vs direction is mostly label algebra.
Experiment 0 · the gate

The label-algebra audit

Which of these comparisons is a measurement at all, and which is arithmetic?

Ridge is linear in Y, so if factor g is an exact linear function of factor f, then their weight vectors are related by a fixed matrix for any model, including a random one. That is §34b, and it silently voided three earlier results in this project. So the audit runs first, on labels only — no activations, no model.

The two comparisons this dataset can genuinely make — speed vs direction and object A vs object B — are exactly the two the findings rest on. That is luck, but it is checked luck.

The instrument

A raw principal angle is worthless

What does "the subspaces are 75° apart" actually mean?

Three things corrupt an angle between two fitted weight subspaces, and each needs its own bracket. This is the transferable part of the work.

  • Ceiling. The same factor fit twice on different scenes does not give the same subspace — measured here at overlap 0.33–0.58, i.e. 40–55°. Nothing can beat that, so the unit is the ceiling, not 0°.
  • Null. Ridge on meaningless labels does not return a random direction; it loads on the activation's dominant variance directions, which is where real factors also load. So the null is a shuffled-label fit (0.005–0.017), not a random subspace (0.0006).
  • Shared noise. Fitting two factors on the same scenes lets one noise realisation push both weight vectors the same way. Every cross-factor number here is fit on disjoint halves of the scenes, which removes that channel and matches the ceiling's sample size at once.

Reported as align = (overlap − null) / (ceiling − null): 0 means as unrelated as the estimator allows, 1 means the same subspace measured twice. The ridge penalty is pinned — §30b showed every INLP "sawtooth" in this project was per-round alpha selection — and the headline is unchanged from alpha 1 to 10,000.

Experiment 1 · geometry

Same factor, different object — the same directions

Does ball A's velocity live where ball B's velocity lives?

The two pools involved are not the same vector: A.pre and B.pre share only 0.066 centred cosine across scenes. And the effect is flat across four orders of magnitude of ridge penalty. Pool similarity and regularisation are both ruled out — but geometry alone cannot rule out coincidence in a 768-dimensional space.

A single local readout reports whichever object sits under the token. There is one code per factor and it is object-agnostic; object identity is carried by which tokens you read, not by which directions. This is a different claim from §18's "objects are separately addressable" — and both are true.

Experiment 4 · the reconciliation

Inside one pool, the two objects are orthogonal

If the code is shared, why did §18 find the objects separable?

Read both velocities from the same pool and they are orthogonal — align −0.03 in A.pre, −0.00 in B.pre, on both encoders. §18b's ~75° on v3, sharper here. The picture that fits everything: the own-object velocity is written in the canonical shared code, while the other object's velocity also reaches these tokens (0.44–0.53) but through a different, orthogonal channel.

Experiment 5 · pooling

A frame-wide pool computes a sum

What does mean-pooling over both objects actually return?

The frame-wide pool carries the sum better than either object-local pool does, and the difference not at all. Applying the A-fitted velocity readout to all.pre and regressing its output on the two true velocities gives near-equal weights on both objects — an unweighted average.

§16 read this as "frame-wide pools lose the objects". They do not lose them — they superpose them. A mean over tokens of an object-agnostic per-token code is a sum over whatever objects are in the pool, and a sum cannot be un-mixed. It also explains why frame-wide pools win on momentum (0.649 vs 0.419): momentum is a sum.

Experiment 6 · the variance basis

Concentration is not redundancy

A claim that fell to its own control, for the same reason as Experiment 7's.

I measured the energy of each factor's ridge weight vector in each principal component, found ~0% in the top two against 46–65% of the activation variance, and concluded PCA truncation would destroy the physics. That measured the estimator, not the model.

In the PC basis, ridge weights are W = Σ [s/(s²+α)] · corr(Y, PC) · PC. The shrinkage factor peaks at s = √α and decays as 1/s — so a ridge probe puts little weight on leading components however much label information they carry.

The estimator-free test: regress the label on each PC's scores alone, out-of-fold OLS, no ridge.

Sixteen removals cost the ridge readout almost nothing (0.954 → 0.904) while collapsing the top-3 readout (0.913 → 0.317). The two measurements name independent properties: concentration — where the primary copy sits in the variance spectrum — and redundancy — how many copies exist. Direction is both concentrated and redundant; speed is neither. "How many dimensions does this variable need" has no single answer, and this dataset gives opposite orderings in the two bases.

What survives is worth more than the withdrawn claim. First: a probe's weight geometry is not a map of where information lives. Ridge puts 4% of its energy in a component that alone predicts the target at R² 0.54. This qualifies my own angle results and Joseph et al.'s C.4 equally — both read geometry off probe weights — and it is why the headline here rests on the functional transfer test, which involves no weight geometry at all. Second, a real estimator-free dissociation: direction rides a leading component (PC1/PC2, 11–29% of variance) while speed rides a low-variance one (PC4/PC5, ~5%), in both models.

One methodological disagreement, and it bears on their central negative

Their subspace null is the analytic random-subspace expectation k_A/d, they report no reliability ceiling, and both probes in a pair are fit on the same data. Measured here, all three choices matter:

bracketvalue
random 2-d subspaces (their baseline's form)0.0006
shuffled-label fit0.012  ~20× higher
split-half ceiling (same factor, twice)0.33–0.58

C.4 concludes "no representational reuse" from a direction→IntPhys overlap of 0.129 against an expected 0.133. With a ceiling near 0.5 rather than 1.0 that conclusion probably survives — but without a ceiling the test cannot separate "no shared structure" from "too noisy to resolve any", and same-data fits bias toward agreement rather than away.

Next

What to ask about the collision itself

Neither prior paper touches contact — both are single-object. Their own limitations say so: "we do not isolate richer physical computations such as contact dynamics."

The binding constraint. Every scene on disk collides, at frame 20.033–20.067, sd 0.0166. So "did a collision occur" and "when" have zero variance — detection and timing need a render. The collision's consequences do not, and those come first.

Ranked. Items 1–4 run on pixels or captures that already exist.

2Causal propagation. Patch A's pre-contact state, read B's post-contact velocity. Does it move as the law requires?~3 minwhether the collision is COMPUTED, or two trajectories are merely tracked

5Contact vs near-miss, matched pairs. Same seed, impact parameter just above/below touching~2 GPU-hcollision DETECTION — the headline question
6Variable contact time (12–36 instead of pinned at 20)~2 GPU-htiming; unlocks time-to-contact, which has no variance anywhere on disk

One trap to design around in #5, and it is fatal if missed: after the near-miss point the two members of a pair diverge in pixels, so a post-contact probe separates them on geometry alone. Restrict the pre-contact probe to frames where the clips are pixel-identical, and verify with a frame-by-frame difference curve — the same lesson that voided an earlier dataset here.

Controls, and what would break this

  • The 24 stationary-B scenes. Every result re-run on the 51 moving-B scenes and holds — transfer A→B velocity 0.956 against own B 0.940, align 1.06 / 1.12, speed-vs-direction align −0.01 to −0.04.
  • The pooling-mask leak (§20). Floors recomputed for the conserving 75: velocity from A.pre sits on 0.312, direction on 0.320, and the frame-wide floors are −0.06 to −0.11 as they must be. No conclusion here is an absolute R² claim — transfer, ablation and angle results are all differences at fixed mask, which a mask cannot manufacture.
  • Sample size. n = 75, 45 train / 30 test. Every geometric number averages 40 disjoint-half repeats; the decodability grid is a single split and carries the usual winner's curse.
  • What would break the headline: a transfer test that succeeds in the swapped condition. It fails at −0.4 to −1.1 everywhere, which is why the claim is about a local shared code rather than a global feature.
